How to make a monthly budget (with a real example in CAD and USD)

Short answer

To make a monthly budget, write down your take-home pay for the month, list your fixed bills, then give every remaining dollar a job (groceries and day-to-day spending, debt payments, savings) until income minus everything equals zero. Use the smallest normal month of pay you can count on, not your best month. Then track what you actually spend for 30 days and adjust, because the first version is always wrong somewhere.

Before you start: 15 minutes of gathering

You can't budget from memory. Before you write a single number, pull up three things:

  • Your last two or three months of bank and credit card statements (download them as PDFs or CSVs).
  • Your most recent pay stubs, so you know your real take-home amount, after tax, benefits and pension or 401(k).
  • Anything you pay once or twice a year: car insurance, a Costco or Amazon membership, property tax, a domain name, school fees.

That last list is where most first budgets break. A $900 insurance renewal in August is a $75-a-month cost, whether or not you plan for it.

Step 1: Pick your income number

Use take-home pay, the amount that actually lands in your account. Then use the smallest normal month, not an average:

  • Paid every two weeks: budget on two paycheques a month. Two months a year you'll get a third one. With a Friday payday starting January 8, 2027, those months are April, October. Decide now where the third cheque goes (debt, a sinking fund, or the emergency fund) so it doesn't quietly disappear.
  • Paid weekly: budget on four paycheques, even in five-Friday months.
  • Paid on the 15th and last day: two cheques, every month. This is the easiest schedule to budget.
  • Irregular income (freelance, tips, commission): take the three lowest months from the last 12 and average them. Anything above that number goes into a holding account and gets assigned the next month.

Step 2: List your fixed bills

Fixed bills are the ones that come with a late fee or a cancellation if you skip them: rent or mortgage, utilities, internet, phone, insurance, transit or car payment, minimum debt payments, childcare, and subscriptions. Write the amount and the due day for each. If something is billed yearly, divide it by 12 and put it under sinking funds in Step 5 instead.

Step 3: Put real numbers on day-to-day spending

This is groceries, gas, eating out, household stuff, personal care and fun money. Don't guess. Add up each category from your last two or three statements and divide by the number of months. If your statements say you spent $612, $540 and $588 on groceries, your grocery line is $580, not the $400 you wish it were. You can aim lower next month once you know the real starting point.

Keep a fun-money line, even a small one. A budget with zero room for a takeout night tends to last about three weeks.

Step 4: Add debt payments

Every debt gets at least its minimum payment. If you have money left after Step 5, extra debt payments go here too. Which debt gets the extra is a separate decision; we walk through it with real numbers in debt snowball vs avalanche.

Step 5: Add savings and sinking funds

Savings is two different jobs. An emergency fund covers things you can't predict (job loss, a burst pipe). Sinking funds cover things you can: Christmas, car repairs, the dentist, the annual renewal you forgot about last year. A simple rule: total up last year's irregular costs, divide by 12, and that's your monthly sinking-fund line. There's a full list of categories with amounts in our sinking funds guide.

Step 6: Make it zero

Add a running "left to assign" column. Start with your income at the top and subtract each line as you go. When you reach the bottom, the number should be exactly $0. If it's negative, cut from the bottom of the list (the least important lines) until it isn't. If it's positive, that money still needs a job: give it to a goal. This is what people mean by a zero-based budget. It doesn't mean you spend everything. It means every dollar, including the ones you save, is assigned on purpose.

What about the 50/30/20 rule?

The 50/30/20 rule says 50% of take-home pay goes to needs, 30% to wants and 20% to savings and extra debt payments. It's a fine sanity check, but don't force your budget to fit it. In the Vancouver example, needs (fixed bills plus groceries) come to $2,953 out of $4,300, or 68.7%. No amount of skipped lattes turns that into 50%. In the Ohio example, needs (fixed bills, groceries and gas) are $4,115 of $5,800, or 70.9%. If your needs are over 60%, the useful question isn't "how do I hit 50/30/20" but "which one big cost (rent, car, phone plan) can I change in the next year?"

Step 7: Track it for 30 days

Your plan is a guess until you compare it with what actually happened. Give each line two more columns, Actual and Difference. Once a week, on the same day (Sunday evening works for most people), open your banking app and add up what you spent in each category. It takes about 10 minutes if you do it weekly and an hour if you leave it to the end of the month.

Step 8: The month-end review

On the last day of the month, answer four questions, in writing:

  1. Which line was furthest off, and was the plan wrong or the spending?
  2. What came up that wasn't on the list at all? (Add it, or add it to a sinking fund.)
  3. Did the third paycheque, refund or bonus get assigned, or did it vanish?
  4. What's one number you'll change for next month?

Expect the first three months to be messy. By month three, your fixed lines should match to the dollar, and the day-to-day lines will be close enough to plan with.

Why first budgets fail

  • Forgetting yearly costs. The car insurance renewal shows up, the budget "breaks," and people quit. Sinking funds fix this.
  • Budgeting groceries from memory. Use your statements.
  • No fun money. Even $50 a month makes the rest easier to stick to.
  • Starting mid-month with no buffer. If you can, start on the 1st, or right after a payday.
  • Two people, two budgets. If you share bills, sit down together for the first version. One person building the budget and the other finding out about it later almost never works.

Paper, spreadsheet or app?

Use the one you'll actually open every week. Paper is slower to update but you see the whole month at a glance, and writing numbers by hand makes people notice them. A spreadsheet does the math for you and is easier to copy month to month. Apps that link to your bank save typing, but they categorize things automatically, and it's easy to stop looking. Plenty of people use a spreadsheet for tracking and a printed one-page plan on the fridge.

Questions people ask

How much should I budget for groceries?

Use your own number, not a national average. Add up grocery spending on your last two or three bank and card statements and divide by the number of months. Start the budget there, then lower it by $25 to $50 a month if you want to bring it down.

What is a zero-based budget?

A zero-based budget gives every dollar of your monthly income a job, including savings, so income minus all the lines equals exactly zero. It doesn't mean spending everything; money you save is assigned to a savings line.

How do I make a budget with irregular income?

Base the budget on the average of your three lowest months from the last year. Put anything you earn above that into a holding account and assign it in the following month, starting with bills and the emergency fund.

Should I budget weekly or monthly?

Plan monthly, because most bills are monthly, but check in weekly. A 10-minute weekly check on the same day each week catches overspending while you can still fix it.

Does the 50/30/20 rule work if I live somewhere expensive?

Often not. In an expensive city, rent alone can be close to half of take-home pay, so needs end up at 60 to 70%. Use 50/30/20 as a reference point and build your actual budget line by line.
