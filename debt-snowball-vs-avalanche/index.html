Debt snowball vs avalanche: which pays off debt faster? (worked example)

Short answer

The debt snowball pays off your smallest balance first; the debt avalanche pays off your highest interest rate first. Either way you pay the minimum on everything else and roll each freed-up payment into the next debt. Avalanche always costs the same or less interest, while snowball gets you a paid-off account sooner: in our worked example ($24,100 across four debts, $400 a month extra), avalanche saved $436.61 and one month, but snowball cleared the first debt in month 3 instead of month 14.

How each method works

Both methods use the same monthly amount. You decide how much you can put toward debt in total, pay every minimum, and send whatever is left to one target debt. When the target is paid off, its whole payment rolls onto the next target. The only difference is the order.

Debt snowballDebt avalanche
Target firstSmallest balanceHighest interest rate
Good atQuick wins, fewer bills to juggle soonerLowest total interest, usually a slightly earlier finish
Weak spotA big high-rate card can sit there growingThe first payoff can take a year or more

The worked example: four debts, $24,100

This is a realistic Canadian mix: a store card, a Visa, a line of credit and a car loan. The minimums add up to $650 a month. The numbers work the same in US dollars.

With $400 a month extra:

  • Snowball pays them off in this order: Store card (month 3), Line of credit (month 9), Visa (month 20), Car loan (month 27). Total interest $3,609.99.
  • Avalanche pays them off in this order: Visa (month 14), Store card (month 15), Line of credit (month 19), Car loan (month 26). Total interest $3,173.38.

So avalanche wins by $436.61 and one month. Snowball gives you your first paid-off account in month 3. On avalanche you're 14 months in before anything is closed, which is a long time to see four balances on every statement.

The bigger lesson: the extra amount matters more than the method

Look at the first two rows. With no extra money (you still roll each freed-up minimum into the next debt), it takes 49 months and about $7,604 in interest either way; the methods are only $38 apart. Adding $200 a month cuts that to 34 to 35 months. Adding $400 cuts it to 26 to 27 months and roughly halves the interest. Picking the "right" method is worth a few hundred dollars here. Finding the extra $400 is worth over $4,000.

If you don't know where an extra $200 or $400 would come from, start with a monthly budget. The money usually turns up in two places: subscriptions and food you didn't plan.

Which one should you pick?

Pick avalanche if the gap between your interest rates is big (say, a 22.99% card next to a 7.99% car loan) and you're the kind of person who can keep going without a quick win. The bigger your highest-rate balance, the more avalanche saves.

Pick snowball if you've started and stopped before, if you have several small balances that clutter your month, or if your rates are all close together. When rates are similar, the interest difference is small and the motivation difference is large.

Check this first: if your smallest balance also has the highest rate, both methods give you the exact same plan. Just start.

A hybrid that works for a lot of people: pay off any debt under about $1,000 first, for the win and one less bill, then switch to avalanche for the rest. In our example, that means the store card first (it's gone in month 3), then the Visa.

How to set up your payoff plan in 20 minutes

  1. List every debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment. Pull these from your latest statements, not memory. Leave out your mortgage; payoff plans are for consumer debt.
  2. Decide your total monthly debt amount: all minimums plus the extra you can commit to every month. Write it down. This number stays the same until you're done.
  3. Number the debts in your chosen order: smallest balance first (snowball) or highest rate first (avalanche).
  4. Pay minimums on everything, and the rest on debt #1. Set up the minimums on autopay so a missed payment can't undo your progress.
  5. Log each payment and the new balance once a month, the day the statement comes in.
  6. When a debt hits zero, add its whole payment to the next one. Don't let the freed-up money drift back into everyday spending. That roll-over is the entire method.

What breaks a debt payoff plan

  • Still using the cards. If the balance you're paying down is also growing, nothing works. Take the card out of your wallet and your phone's saved payments.
  • No buffer. The first surprise car repair goes straight back on the Visa. Many people keep a small starter buffer, $500 to $1,000, before putting everything into debt.
  • Forgetting yearly bills. These are what usually send people back to the card. Sinking funds handle them.
  • A balance transfer with no plan. A low promotional rate helps only if you pay the balance down before the promo ends. Check the transfer fee and the rate afterward.

If the numbers don't work at all

If your minimums alone are more than you can pay, a payoff method won't fix it. Call your lenders before you miss a payment; many have hardship options. Free, non-profit credit counselling can also help you look at the whole picture: in Canada, the Credit Counselling Society; in the US, agencies that belong to the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC). Questions people ask

Is the debt snowball or avalanche better?

Avalanche (highest interest rate first) always costs the same or less interest. Snowball (smallest balance first) gives you a paid-off account sooner, which helps many people keep going. In our example with $24,100 of debt and $400 a month extra, avalanche saved $436.61 and one month.

Do I include my mortgage in the debt snowball?

Usually not. Snowball and avalanche plans are for consumer debt: credit cards, lines of credit, car loans, personal loans and student loans. Your mortgage stays on its regular payment.

What if two debts have the same interest rate?

Pay the smaller balance first. You get the payoff sooner and the interest cost is the same.

Should I stop saving while I pay off debt?

Most people keep a small buffer, often $500 to $1,000, so a surprise expense doesn't go back on a credit card. Beyond that it depends on your rates and situation.

How much faster does an extra payment pay off debt?

In our worked example, minimums alone took 49 months. Adding $200 a month cut it to 34 to 35 months, and $400 a month cut it to 26 to 27 months, roughly halving the interest.
