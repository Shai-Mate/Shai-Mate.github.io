Pet Holiday Safety Checklist for Dogs & Cats

The holidays bring rich food, new plants, sparkly decorations and a house full of guests, and all of it is new to your pet. This checklist pulls together guidance from veterinary groups and the FDA so you can pet-proof the house in one pass.

1. Food and treats

  • Chocolate: treat all chocolate as off limits. Baking and dark chocolate are the most dangerous.
  • Xylitol (also called birch sugar): found in some sugar-free candy, gum, mints, baked goods and peanut butter. In dogs it can cause a sudden drop in blood sugar, so check labels.
  • Grapes, raisins, onions and rich table scraps like gravy, fat and skin. Cooked bones can cause choking or blockages.
  • Alcohol and unbaked yeast dough. Keep drinks and rising dough out of reach.
  • Wrapped gifts with food inside: pets can smell through paper, so keep them off the floor.

2. Holiday plants

  • Lilies: many lilies in holiday bouquets are highly toxic to cats and can cause kidney failure. Keep them out of a cat home entirely.
  • Mistletoe and holly: can cause stomach upset; large amounts of mistletoe are more serious.
  • Poinsettias: usually cause mild to moderate mouth and stomach irritation, but still keep them out of reach. Faux plants are the easy fix.

3. The tree and decorations

  • Secure the tree so a climbing cat or excited dog can't tip it.
  • Cover the tree water and skip water additives.
  • Skip tinsel in a cat home. Swallowed tinsel, ribbon and string can block the gut and may need surgery.
  • Hang breakable and food-based ornaments up high; tidy cords and batteries.
  • Never leave a pet alone with a lit candle; keep liquid potpourri out of reach.

4. Guests and parties

  • Set up a quiet room with water, bed and litter or a familiar blanket.
  • Ask guests not to feed scraps, and watch the door so no one slips out.
  • Make sure your pet's ID tag and microchip details are current.

5. Travelling or using a sitter?

Leave one sheet with feeding times, medications, your vet and the nearest emergency clinic, and a way to reach you. If your pet is on regular meds, a free pet medication log on the fridge stops missed or doubled doses, and a vaccination record helps if you board.

If something gets eaten

Call your veterinarian or an emergency vet right away. You can also call ASPCA Animal Poison Control at 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661 (a fee may apply).
